# Can a third vessel help predict how an encounter unfolds?

**X51 · Multi-vessel behaviour understanding from AIS · 6 November 2026 · Conditional pilot**

Consider a ferry crossing a channel while two cargo vessels approach. A pairwise model sees the ferry and one cargo vessel at a time. A scene model sees all three at the same instant. A useful test of behaviour understanding is whether seeing the third track helps anticipate **which two vessels will pass closest and how their motion will change**. Both outcomes can be checked against subsequent AIS reports. This gives a traffic analyst an offline replay with a forecast, the observed outcome and examples where the extra context helped or hurt.

## What would count as a contribution?

The [Consensus literature review](../Evaluate%20a%20three-student%20undergraduate%20FYP%20proposal-%20a%20model%20capable%20of%20multi-ve.md) accompanies this brief with paper-level citations and a comparison of multi-ship architectures.

**Primary question.** Given ten minutes of AIS history for three underway vessels, does a model using all three histories rank the pair with the smallest separation over the *next* ten minutes more accurately than the same encoder scoring each pair independently? Rank all three pairs in both conditions. A secondary behaviour measure checks how accurately each model predicts each vessel's signed course change over the horizon. A correctly ranked pair can follow from geometry alone. Improvement on turns and on scenes where independent forecasts disagree would give stronger evidence that the model uses the surrounding traffic.

The gap is a **candidate evaluation question**. A convincing result could be that pairwise geometry suffices except in a small, identifiable subset. Traffic density, channel bends and regular ferry routes can make neighbouring motion predictive without demonstrating that one vessel caused another to turn. AIS supports claims about motion among reporting vessels. Intent, legal responsibility and collision avoidance require other evidence.

## Build the reference before training

1. **Start in one waterway.** The Piraeus authors describe 32 kinematic CSVs with anonymised vessel IDs, timestamps, positions, speed and course. Table 1 lists 244 million dynamic records and CC BY-NC-SA 4.0. Use one area and a bounded date range. A separately packaged release lists downloadable position archives by year. Verify the chosen files and reuse terms before redistribution. Danish historical AIS is a possible independent waterway after the primary test works. [Dataset paper and licence table](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.dib.2021.107782) · [Piraeus data access](https://datascience.cs.unipi.gr/piraeus-ais/) · [Yearly archive listing](https://zenodo.org/records/10006470) · [Danish Maritime Authority](https://www.dma.dk/safety-at-sea/navigational-information/ais-data)
2. **Select scenes from the past only.** At fixed issue times, use a predefined local radius, recent valid reports and an underway criterion to identify triplets. Start with exactly three eligible vessels. Keep routine traffic and easy encounters in the test. Report exclusions, scenes with extra vessels and how the radius affects coverage. Group overlapping windows of the same encounter before splitting. Feed age-of-last-report and missingness to every learned comparator.
3. **Derive outcomes from later reports.** Synchronise future positions to a common clock using bounded interpolation between observed fixes. For each pair, take the minimum simultaneous separation in local metric coordinates over the forecast horizon. A valid ranking needs adequate future coverage for **all three** vessels. Record the smallest and second-smallest distances. Flag near-ties using an uncertainty tolerance chosen on development data and vary the grid and interpolation limit. Score signed course change with wrapped angles and exclude low-speed segments where course is unstable. These are AIS-derived references.
4. **Protect the test.** Use later, non-overlapping traffic episodes for testing, purge adjacent windows spanning the split, and report repeated vessel or route identities. Keep the test waterway untouched for any transfer claim. Report top-pair accuracy with ties handled explicitly, error in predicted minimum separation in metres, circular course-change error, and paired intervals resampled by traffic episode or day. Show routine and manoeuvring scenes separately alongside their frequencies.

## One model, three individual experiments

Use a shared track encoder and a small interaction layer. Each vessel contributes motion and report timing. Each pair contributes separation, relative bearing and closing speed. The interaction layer lets the representation of A–B depend on C. Use a symmetric pair score so swapping A and B changes nothing. A frozen general time-series encoder with a small trainable head is a feasible foundation-model route to test. Compare it with an AIS-trained compact temporal encoder and the same architecture with random weights. Only claim an advantage from pretraining if that comparison supports it. [MOMENT-small checkpoint](https://huggingface.co/AutonLab/MOMENT-1-small)

| Member | Question and controlled experiment | Result |
|---|---|---|
| A — modelling individual motion | Does pretraining improve per-vessel course and short-horizon position forecasts over constant velocity and a compact supervised temporal model at matched training-vessel counts? Rank pairs implied by the independent forecasts. | Motion errors and downstream pair-ranking accuracy versus training data and compute. |
| B — modelling vessel interactions | Compare current nearest pair, constant-velocity closest approach clipped to the forecast horizon, a learned pair-only scorer, and a full-scene scorer with matched capacity. Keep the candidate pairs fixed when masking third-vessel context. Swap third tracks between density- and route-matched scenes as a diagnostic. | Paired difference in top-pair accuracy and separation error. Analyse when the third vessel changes a correct or incorrect ranking. |
| C — reliability with partial observations | Delay or thin the third vessel's history while retaining its identity and the original candidate set. Compare a learned confidence estimate with a simple margin between the two leading pair scores. | Error versus fraction of scenes answered, broken down by report age, near-ties and traffic regime. Test a later period first and another waterway if available. |

## Decision against the FYP criteria

**Pilot before shortlisting.** X51 fits the team's interest in interaction modelling better than a single-vessel forecast. It costs more data preparation than [X49](x49-ais-foundation-forecast-stale-tracks.md). [X30](x30-sparse-vessel-colocation.md) studies whether two vessels stayed together under sparse observations. [X50](x50-ais-foundation-fishing-onset.md) studies when a fishing episode becomes detectable. X51 makes its own testable claim about the *incremental information in a third ship's track*.

In a two-week pilot, build triplets for one area, count independent episodes by day, measure how many retain all three future tracks, and inspect examples where nearest-now and constant-velocity rankings disagree. Quantify sensitivity to interpolation and near-ties. Run both cheap baselines before loading one checkpoint on the available machine. Proceed if label rankings are stable and the retained episodes permit useful uncertainty bounds on the pairwise-versus-scene difference. If rankings are mostly predetermined at issue time, restrict the scientific question to a prospectively defined ambiguous subset and report its prevalence. If too few independent examples survive, keep X49 as the lower-risk alternative.

The project uses existing trajectories, objective outcomes, one small shared model and three linked studies within the proposed 6–8 months. The data records and primary papers were checked. Scene yield, memory, runtime and gains over pairwise baselines are unmeasured. The [module criteria](../../../research-module-and-team-criteria.md) call for resolving these gates before scoring.
