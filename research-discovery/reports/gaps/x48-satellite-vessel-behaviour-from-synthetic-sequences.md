# Vessel movement behaviour from satellite image sequences trained with AIS-driven simulation

**ID:** X48 · **Area:** Satellite video, computer vision, synthetic-to-real transfer · **Status:** Higher novelty potential, high evaluation risk  
**Evidence checked:** 4 November 2026. Links point to original dataset pages, code repositories or papers. This is a proposal appraisal, not an implemented result or an exhaustive proof of novelty.

## 1. What the project would do

The intended input is a **time-ordered optical satellite image sequence** containing a detected and tracked vessel. The model predicts an **observable movement pattern** from the images or image-derived tracks: transit, stop/slow movement, turn, approach to another ship, repeated movement in a small area, or crossing a defined boundary. It should work without that vessel's AIS at inference. AIS is used during development to supply plausible trajectories, create provisional motion labels and drive synthetic image sequences.

The core chain is: AIS trajectories → operational event labels → optical satellite-style frames at known times → detector/tracker or track extraction → temporal classifier → real-video transfer test. A continuous movie is **not required**; correctly timestamped frames at an interval that actually captures the event can suffice. The time spacing, sensor resolution and total observation duration matter more than cinematic smoothness. No generative AI is required to place vessel models/sprites into scenes; a conventional graphics renderer can generate the first dataset. A small amount of real satellite video is needed to test whether synthetic training transfers.

This is **not** a model of illegality, actual intent, fishing gear use or cargo transfer. “Approach” is a measurable motion pattern; “transfer” needs independent evidence. A detected vessel without AIS may receive a prediction, but an AIS-derived reference test proves only performance on AIS-reporting vessels whose AIS was hidden from the model.

## 2. The research question and sharp gap

**Question:** Can AIS-driven, sensor-aware synthetic optical sequences train a model to recognize specified vessel movement patterns in **real satellite video**, with AIS absent from model input, better than simple image-track kinematic baselines?

**Potential contribution:** A reproducible synthetic-to-real experiment that varies image resolution, frame interval, missed detections, viewing geometry and track duration; tests which classes remain observable; and calibrates uncertain predictions. It is **not** enough to render AIS tracks and report high accuracy on other renders. That would only show a classifier can read the simulation and its rule-generated labels.

**Closest novelty assessment:** In the source set below, AIS-driven maritime 3D scenes, synthetic ship imagery, satellite-video tracking, AIS behaviour classification and satellite snapshot activity recognition all exist. We did **not verify** a directly matching public study that trains with AIS-driven synthetic **orbital-looking vessel sequences** and validates a multi-class movement classifier on real satellite video. That is a provisional gap, not a “first ever” claim. The closest papers must be checked again during the final literature review.

**No currently identified dataset combines all three requirements:** real satellite video of enough ship movement, AIS association to those exact video sequences, and independently verified movement-event labels. SAT-MTB/OOTB can still support a narrower evaluation if the footage itself visibly contains turns/approaches and a transparent motion label can be derived from its **own annotated image tracks**. That is an image-track operational label, not proof of a real-world intent. The existence of videos permits verification **only for classes visibly represented over adequate durations**; it does not verify unobservable or absent events.

**AIS labels have a specific strength and limit.** The [Composite Maritime Events paper](https://cer.iit.demokritos.gr/publications/papers/2019/pitsikalis-CERMM.pdf) was peer reviewed and checked selected first-month recognized events with expert feedback. For example, its expert-feedback table reported 52 true positives and 2 false positives for `rendezVous`. The authors could not calculate recall without complete ground truth; loitering was not among the expert-checked classes. Its event rules can train a synthetic-image model or provide a **rule-agreement test**, but they cannot independently verify what real satellite footage shows. Apply equivalent rules to AIS paired with a real image only when an actual time/location match exists; do not join unrelated Brest events to SAT-MTB clips.

## 5. Study design that tests the actual idea

1. **Select feasible classes from real footage first.** Audit ship sequences and timestamps in SAT-MTB/OOTB. Count complete windows for turns, stops, approaches and zone crossings. Loitering is admissible only when a sequence spans the prespecified duration; a 10–30 second clip cannot verify an hours-long definition.
2. **Define reference labels before modelling.** For the real test, derive explicit kinematic classes from annotated image-track centroids when image evidence suffices, then independently spot-check annotation errors if feasible. If labels come from the same thresholds as a baseline, report the circularity and do not call the result semantic ground truth. Store an `unresolvable` class.
3. **Generate synthetic optical sequences.** Sample AIS track segments with vessel-disjoint splits; choose vessel sizes, orientation, realistic sensor ground sampling distance, blur, sea texture, clouds/haze and detection omissions. Apply real time intervals rather than arbitrary speed-up. Generate image frames, boxes/IDs, track quality metadata and weak event labels. Begin with simple compositing/Blender rather than a diffusion or full physics renderer.
4. **Model and compare levels of input.** First test rules and compact ML on perfect AIS coordinates, then on noisy AIS coordinates, then on tracks extracted from synthetic frames, and finally on tracks extracted from real videos. A lightweight pretrained detector/tracker plus small temporal head is a plausible starting point. This ladder determines whether rendering trains a useful visual representation or merely adds complexity to a track-only problem.
5. **Synthetic-to-real transfer.** Hold out real videos for final testing. Compare synthetic-only training, simple domain randomisation, optional small real-video calibration/fine-tuning if labels are legitimately available, and track-only baselines. Do not tune on the final test sequences.
6. **Reliability.** Vary frame cadence, sequence duration, spatial resolution, missed detections, vessel scale and multi-vessel density. Calibrate an abstention decision for cases with too little evidence.

The proposed first version is **optical**. SAR simulation requires radar-scattering realism and a different imaging model; MMDEC's Sentinel-1 snapshots are not a drop-in SAR video test. A combined optical/SAR generator would materially increase scope.

**Compute choice:** Render a bounded set of cropped, time-stamped frames from AIS trajectories; cache detections or track embeddings; train a compact temporal head on short windows. Compositing or a standard 3D renderer can generate first-pass frames without a generative model. A pretrained detector can reduce GPU cost. A diffusion video generator, high-fidelity SAR simulator, full-resolution satellite-video transformer or multi-sensor end-to-end model would make this a substantially larger project. Actual memory and training time must be measured in the pilot rather than asserted.

## 6. Sentinel-2 snapshots versus satellite video

The supplied **“Reading ship behaviour from free satellite photos”** PDF proposes a **different, narrower study**. Its input is one Sentinel-1 SAR or Sentinel-2 optical image crop, not a sequence; its proposed outputs are parked/slow/underway, single/alongside/nearby, and confidence or “I don't know.” It would match ships in free coastal satellite images to AIS at acquisition time, derive weak motion/proximity labels, train a small image model and test on unseen regions. Its illustrative pilot asks whether enough images align with AIS within roughly 15 minutes, whether clear encounter examples exist and whether two large nearby ships remain visually separable. Those are **proposal assumptions to test**, not established dataset facts or validated thresholds.

That snapshot plan has three potential member studies: (1) motion-state recognition against wake and physics-based speed estimates; (2) alongside/nearby recognition and its resolution-dependent size limit; and (3) AIS label-error, region transfer and confidence. This is not the same as AIS-driven synthetic **video** generation, which adds motion across frames, tracking and a synthetic-to-real test. A satellite-video model is not automatically better: if real clips are too short for the desired behaviour, the simpler snapshot task may be more honest.

Sentinel-2 snapshots can support vessel detection, rough type, wake/speed cues and spatial arrangements in certain conditions. They generally cannot reconstruct an individual ship's short turn, approach or loitering history from revisit-spaced acquisitions. A single image may weakly suggest motion; it does not show a trajectory through time. At 10 m pixels a 100 m ship occupies only about ten pixels along its length; very small boats may occupy one or two pixels. Sentinel-2 optical images are cloud-limited; Sentinel-1 SAR works in cloud/night but is a different visual domain. The [Sentinel-2 band-offset speed study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6651096/) and [single-image AIS-linked classification](https://www.eurasip.org/Proceedings/Eusipco/eusipco2019/Proceedings/papers/1570532157.pdf) are important precedents for the narrower motion-state claim. [Ballinger's PlanetScope encounter study](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.07607) is a close precedent for the alongside claim, although it used commercial imagery.

The snapshot proposal's phrase “parked (anchored or moored)” is too strong if based only on speed or a single image: slow motion, drifting, docking and anchoring can look similar. Its suggested close-and-slow AIS encounter rule is also a **derived event definition**, not a confirmed meeting or transfer. The Brest composite-event labels were expert-checked only for selected recognized examples, not fully independently verified for all activities. Train/test region separation, AIS/image timing-error audit, cloud filtering and vessel-size breakdown would be essential. A freely downloadable, adequately large **alongside** image test set has not yet been secured.

The satellite-video proposal therefore needs **dense enough real sequences** and matching synthetic sampling. A few correctly spaced frames may suffice for a turn or approach; long loitering needs a long observation. Neither synthetic continuous video nor a large video model repairs missing temporal information in Sentinel-2 revisit data.

## 7. Evaluation and claim boundaries

- **Splits:** separate AIS vessels, voyages, dates and regions across synthetic train/validation/test. Group all frames and overlapping crops of a real video in one partition. Check whether SAT-MTB and OOTB contain re-used source clips before combining them.
- **Metrics:** macro-F1 and per-class precision/recall; PR-AUC for rare approach/loitering events; event onset delay; calibration/Brier score; risk–coverage with abstention. Bootstrap by vessel/video, not frame.
- **Tracking metrics:** detection recall, ID switches, track fragmentation and position error. Report event accuracy on oracle boxes/tracks and on model-extracted tracks so visual failure is not hidden.
- **Baselines:** image-track threshold rules; shallow kinematic classifier; small temporal model on extracted tracks; frozen video features with a simple head; synthetic-only versus synthetic plus legitimate real training labels.
- **Ablations:** remove rendering, remove sensor-specific degradations, remove real backgrounds, vary cadence/resolution/duration, remove temporal context (single frame), and compare with perfectly known coordinates.
- **Bias/label checks:** AIS vessels may be larger or follow different routes than silent/small vessels. Synthetic sea and ship sprites can create shortcut cues. Use background and vessel-style holdouts; inspect whether the model relies on image appearance instead of motion.
- **Valid claims:** high synthetic-test accuracy validates the simulator-defined task. A real-video test with image-derived operational labels supports real visual motion-pattern recognition for those classes. It does **not** independently verify intention, illegal activity, true loitering beyond clip duration, or generalization to all AIS-silent vessels.

## 8. Three connected research contributions

| Member | Question | Own experiment and assessable result |
|---|---|---|
| A — event synthesis and observability | Which AIS-derived movement events survive realistic orbital sampling? | Build a documented trajectory-to-frame generator; vary resolution/cadence/window duration; compare perfect-track and simulated-visual observability, with a reproducible dataset manifest. |
| B — visual sequence recognition | Does visual temporal learning outperform simple rules on the same supported classes? | Compare detector/tracker plus compact temporal models with image-track rules and feature ML; quantify error propagation from detection and tracking. |
| C — synthetic-to-real reliability | Which simulation choices improve held-out real video transfer, and when should the model abstain? | Run synthetic/real domain ablations, calibration and failure analysis on unchanged video splits; report transfer gap and risk–coverage. |

Each member owns a testable claim, baseline and failure analysis. A video viewer is a demonstration, not a research contribution.

## 9. Feasibility and go/no-go gates

**Conditional go only after a real-video label/coverage audit.** The synthetic pipeline and small temporal models are plausible with one modest GPU and bounded image patches. Full-resolution orbital video training, diffusion-generated imagery, a new SAR renderer, and large video transformers are outside the sensible first scope for this team. The main risk is **real evaluation evidence**, not raw compute.

**What the project can honestly deliver if real labels stay scarce:** a trained model, a simulator, synthetic-test scores and a carefully labelled small real-video pilot. That is a legitimate **simulation and feasibility result**, but it is weaker than a validated general-purpose classifier for every ship in satellite feeds. If the pilot contains only turns and transit, report only those classes. A real no-AIS vessel prediction demonstrates that inference runs without AIS; its correctness still needs an independent reference.

1. Open the SAT-MTB archive linked by its official page and OOTB. Confirm legal download, ship clip counts, timestamps, multi-ship scenes and class-supporting durations. The SAT-MTB Zenodo page was not accessible to this source check, so access remains an explicit gate.
2. Define two or three observable movement classes with enough real examples. Require a vessel/video-disjoint held-out real test and an `unknown` option. If real class support fails, do not claim a satellite-video behaviour classifier has been validated.
3. Train the simple image-track baseline before building the renderer. Show that synthetic imagery offers a measurable benefit on real visual inputs, or reframe the study around observability/synthetic-to-real failure.
4. Keep stronger outcomes—actual AIS-silent ship generalisation, illegal-vessel detection and long-duration loitering—outside the central claim until independent labelled evidence exists.

**Decision:** This idea offers **more apparent novelty than the radar version**, because the checked satellite literature is concentrated on detection, type, tracking, AIS prediction and snapshots. It is also **riskier**: real satellite-video behaviour labels and sufficient event duration are not secured. If those fail the pilot, the radar proposal has a clearer paired-sensor path through HAXR, even though its novelty is narrower.
